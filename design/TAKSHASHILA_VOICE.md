# The Takshashila voice

A working guide to how Pranay Kotasthane and Nitin Pai write, drawn from a
close read of their recent output: Anticipating the Unintended (#348–#359),
Nitin Pai's Mint column "The Intersection" (2026), Pranay's ThePrint columns,
the Takshashila discussion document on gallium and germanium (2026-09), and
the India Critical Minerals Dashboard. Everything below is pattern, not
transcript. Use it to write, not to quote.

## What the voice is for

Both authors write to help a busy, intelligent reader think about a public
problem correctly. Not to persuade by feeling, not to impress, not to
entertain first. The reader is assumed to be a peer who lacks time, not
ability. Every piece exists to leave that reader with a framework they can
reuse and a position they can defend in a meeting.

## The moves, in the order they usually appear

1. **Open on the concrete trigger, dated.** A tweet from a former chief
   minister last week. A regulator's announcement on 15 September. An export
   notice in April 2025. The first sentence names a real event with a real
   date and a real actor. Abstractions come later, if at all.

2. **State the stakes and the plan in one breath.** "Since this technical
   issue is an important one, more so given X, I am attempting an explainer
   that spans A, B and C." The reader is told what they are about to get and
   why it is worth their time. No suspense.

3. **Disclose the prior.** Pranay does this explicitly: "A quick disclosure
   before I begin. My bias is in favour of…" followed immediately by what the
   bias is *not*. The distinction between two things a lazy reader would
   conflate is often the whole argument.

4. **Background, briskly.** Facts in order, each with its source: "According
   to PRS…", "RBI's latest annual report on municipal finances makes four
   points…", "USGS data shows…". Numbers always carry their denominator and
   their condition. Never a bare figure.

5. **The distinction.** The signature move. "Royalty is a contractual payment,
   not a tax." "Higher revenues and freedom to increase tax rates are not the
   same demand." "The two risks are not the same." "That's true, and also
   beside the point." Find the two things everyone is treating as one, pull
   them apart, and the problem reorganises itself.

6. **Name the concept, define it in one sentence, use it.** "A positive
   externality is the gap between…" "Leverage is a product of three factors…"
   Terms of art are welcome once earned. Jargon without a definition is not.

7. **A framework the reader can carry away.** Three metrics. Seven factors and
   a 2×2. Three horizons. Numbered, ordered, and each item does distinct work.
   "First… Second… Third, and most demanding…" Nitin's programmes are almost
   always three lines of effort across three time horizons.

8. **The precedent.** The 1956 Freight Equalisation Scheme. Deng's
   appointocracy. Meiji Japan. "This has happened before." History is used as
   evidence for a mechanism, never as decoration.

9. **The counterintuitive finding, flagged as such.** "This is the
   counterintuitive finding." "Our root cause analysis is wrong." "Counting is
   always political." One sentence, plainly stated, that inverts the reader's
   default.

10. **The India-facing close.** Every piece ends with what New Delhi, the
    Union, a ministry, or Indian firms should do, and when. Balanced between
    two errors: "The objective cannot be X… But neither can it be Y." Then the
    recommendation, and often a one-line aphorism to carry out of the room:
    "Trust compounds." "Nothing is critical if everything is critical."

## Sentence-level habits

- Declaratives. Short verdict sentences after long explanatory ones: "That
  assumption no longer looks as safe as it once did." "Both carry costs."
- "The Union", not "the Centre". "New Delhi", "Beijing", "Washington" as
  actors. "Per cent" spelled out in newsletter and ThePrint copy; Nitin uses
  the symbol in Mint.
- First person is allowed and honest: "I think", "My priority is", "I can
  think of three ways". In institutional copy, "we" for India and for
  Takshashila.
- Rhetorical questions as section heads: "So, how do we think about this
  issue?" "What's the way forward?" "What does reform entail?"
- Dry wit, sparingly, and always in service of a point: "Welcome back,
  retrospective taxation." "This is a gem of a policyWTF." "Forget
  governments." Never whimsy for its own sake.
- Analogies come from public life and economics: the farming community that
  buys its tractors abroad, the nation as your child as well as your mother,
  running where the ball is headed. Not from the kitchen.
- Precision over hedging. "This analysis is incorrect." "The answer cannot
  simply be…" When uncertain, say what is unknown and why: "Only if USGS
  published annual consumption data would it be easier to evaluate…"
- Vocabulary that recurs and is meant: leverage, weaponisation, chokepoint,
  economic statecraft, geopolitical contestation, trade-off, incentives,
  alignment, optionality, state capacity, cost, exposure, dependence,
  resilience, substitutability, asymmetry, drop-in, qualification.

## What the voice never does

- Never opens with a throat-clear or a definition of the topic.
- Never says "it is worth noting", "essentially", "simply put", "in other
  words". The repo's copy lint already bans these; the voice bans them by
  temperament.
- Never gives a number without its source, unit, denominator or condition.
- Never uses "you" to chivvy the reader ("tap here!", "isn't that
  amazing?"). The reader is addressed as a colleague, which means being told
  things, not coaxed.
- Never ends on a summary of what was just said. It ends on what to do.
- Never lets an alternative sound free. Every option "moves the cost
  somewhere else", and the piece says where.

## Applying it to this walkthrough

The walkthrough is an explainer in the sense Pranay uses the word: a
technical issue the reader needs to hold correctly before a decision. So:

- The landing opens on the April 2025 notice and what it did to production
  lines, then states the three questions the walkthrough answers, in order.
- Each frame's card is a paragraph that a Takshashila researcher would write:
  the mechanism, the number with its condition, the cost, the named example.
- The heat frame carries the distinction that matters for policy: the notice
  restricted the 1–4 per cent additive, not the 30 per cent bulk.
- Chapter 5 does the India-facing close across three horizons and names who
  ships what.
- The whole thing stays under 320 characters a frame, so every sentence has
  to carry a fact or a distinction. That constraint suits the voice.
